What every code on your payslip means

UK payslips are dense with abbreviations and almost none of them are explained on the slip itself. Here is the lot.

Gross pay
Everything you earned before any deduction — salary, overtime, bonus, commission. The headline figure, and not one you ever receive.
Taxable pay
Gross pay minus anything that comes off before tax, such as pension contributions under net pay arrangement or salary sacrifice. This, not gross, is what income tax is worked out on.
Net pay also: take-home
What actually reaches your bank. Gross minus tax, NI, pension, student loan and any other deduction. The take-home pay calculator works all of them out together, and the hourly wage calculator converts it to an hourly figure.
PAYE Pay As You Earn
Income tax collected by your employer each payday rather than in a lump at year end. The amount is driven entirely by your tax code — the income tax calculator works it through on a salary.
Tax code
Tells your employer how much you can earn tax-free. The number is the allowance with its last digit dropped — 1257L means £12,570 a year. A K prefix reverses it: the figure is added to taxable pay instead. The tax code checker explains any code.
NI / NIC National Insurance Contributions
A second tax on earnings, funding the State Pension and some benefits. Unlike income tax it is worked out per pay period and never reconciled across the year, so an irregular month can cost more than a steady one. Work yours out with the National Insurance calculator.
NI category letter
A single letter setting which NI rates apply. A is most employees. B is the married women's reduced rate. C is over State Pension age — no employee NI. H apprentices under 25, M under 21, V qualifying veterans, Z under 21 deferring. The employer side of the same contribution is in the employer cost calculator.
NI number e.g. QQ 12 34 56 A
Your permanent reference with HMRC and DWP. Two letters, six digits, one letter. Wrong or missing, and contributions may not credit to your record.
SL Student Loan
Repayment of Plan 1, 2, 4 or 5. Taken as a percentage of earnings above a threshold, never of the whole salary. You may see the plan number, as SL1 or SL2. The student loan repayment calculator covers all five plans.
PGL Postgraduate Loan
A separate deduction from the undergraduate one, with its own threshold and rate. If you have both, both come off — they do not replace each other.
EE / ER employee / employer
Which side a contribution comes from. EE pension is your money; ER pension is your employer's and never reduces your net pay.
AVC Additional Voluntary Contribution
Extra pension saving on top of the standard contribution, usually attracting the same tax relief — see the pension tax relief calculator.
Salary sacrifice often: SAL SAC, SS
You formally give up salary in exchange for a benefit — pension, an EV, childcare. Your gross pay drops, so you save income tax and National Insurance, which plain pension contributions do not. Compare the two with the salary sacrifice calculator and the pension contribution calculator.
Net pay arrangement
Pension taken from pay before income tax, so relief is immediate and at your full rate. Contrast relief at source, where the scheme reclaims 20% and higher-rate taxpayers must claim the rest themselves.
BIK Benefit in Kind
Something your employer gives you that is not cash — a company car, medical cover, a gym membership. Taxable, and usually collected by reducing your tax code rather than as a payslip line. Price one with the benefit in kind calculator or the company car tax calculator.
P11D
The annual return of benefits in kind. Your copy tells you what was reported, which is what moved your tax code.
SSP Statutory Sick Pay
The legal minimum when you are off sick, paid by your employer. Many contracts pay more — that extra is occupational or company sick pay. The Statutory Sick Pay calculator gives the statutory figure.
SMP / SPP / ShPP / SAP
Statutory Maternity, Paternity, Shared Parental and Adoption Pay. Each has its own rate and qualifying rules, and each is taxable like ordinary pay. The maternity pay calculator covers SMP in full.
AEO Attachment of Earnings Order
A court order requiring your employer to deduct money and pay a creditor directly. Your employer must comply and cannot refuse.
DEA Direct Earnings Attachment
Like an AEO but issued by the DWP rather than a court, to recover overpaid benefits. No court hearing is needed.
CMS Child Maintenance Service
A deduction order for child maintenance, collected through payroll.
YTD Year To Date
The running total since 6 April. The single most useful column on the slip — it is what lets you check the year is adding up rather than just this month.
Payroll number also: employee number
Your employer's internal reference. Nothing to do with HMRC, and not transferable between jobs.
Tax period e.g. M07, W31
Which month or week of the tax year this payment falls in. M01 is April, because the tax year starts on 6 April. M12 is March.
BACS
The system that moves the money. A BACS run takes three working days, which is why payday is fixed well before it.
Pro rata
Reduced in proportion — for part-time hours, or a month you did not work in full. A pro-rata salary is the full-time figure scaled, not a separate rate — the pro rata salary calculator does the scaling, and the part-month salary calculator handles a month you only partly worked.
Arrears
Pay owed from an earlier period and paid now. It is taxed in the period it is paid, which can push a single month into a higher band even though the work was spread out. The bonus tax calculator and the overtime tax calculator both show that effect.
Holiday accrued
Leave earned but not yet taken. On leaving, untaken accrued holiday is normally paid out and is taxable as ordinary pay. If you were made redundant, the redundancy pay calculator covers what is and is not taxed.
P45 / P60
P45 when you leave a job — pay and tax so far, for your next employer. P60 at year end — the totals for the whole year, from every employer you had. Keep both — and if too much was taken, the tax refund calculator works out what is owed back.

The line worth checking first is YTD. A single month can look wrong and be right — National Insurance is calculated per period and never smoothed, so a bonus month always looks punitive. Income tax is cumulative and self-corrects. If the year-to-date figures are right, the month almost certainly is too.
